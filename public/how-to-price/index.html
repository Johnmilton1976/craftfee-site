How to price on Etsy: start from the margin you want

Most sellers multiply their material cost by two or three and hope. That produces a number that has nothing to do with profit, and it breaks the moment Etsy runs a sale or an Offsite Ads order arrives. Here is the method that works backwards instead.

Why the multiplier method fails

Doubling the cost feels safe. But it ignores the thing that actually decides whether you make money: what is left after every fee. Two items with the same cost and the same multiplier can deliver completely different margins if one is cheap and one is expensive, because Etsy's flat fees weigh far more on small numbers.

Worse, the multiplier gives you no answer to the only question that matters when a sale comes round: how low can I go before this stops being worth doing?

The formula

For a US seller, the percentage fees that scale with price come to 9.5% — the 6.5% transaction fee plus 3% payment processing. There are also two flat charges totalling $0.45 — the $0.20 listing fee plus the $0.25 fixed processing charge.

So, to hit a target margin measured against revenue:

Price = (cost + 0.45) ÷ (1 − 0.095 − target margin)
In non-US currency or a country with a regulatory fee, replace 0.095 with your combined percentage fees and 0.45 with your own flat charges.

Two worked examples with a $12 material cost:

  • 40% margin: (12 + 0.45) ÷ 0.505 = $24.65
  • 50% margin: (12 + 0.45) ÷ 0.405 = $30.74

That is a multiplier of roughly 2.05× and 2.56× respectively. Notice both are within the range sellers already use — but now you know which multiplier delivers which margin, instead of guessing.

Two things worth noticing. A 30% margin needs a much higher price than most sellers would guess on cheap items. And the gap between 30% and 50% widens as cost rises — the more expensive your materials, the more the target margin costs you in price.

Do not add fees to the price. A common shortcut is "add 10% for Etsy". That under-recovers, because the fee is charged on the raised price too. Percentage fees apply to the price you charge, so covering them requires division, not addition.

How much can you discount?

Discounts come straight out of margin, and Etsy keeps charging its percentage on the reduced price. Take the $24.65 item above, priced for a 40% margin on a $12 cost:

At half price this item sells at a loss, even before accounting for your time. Most sellers discover this only after the sale has ended. A practical rule: decide your maximum discount before you create it, and pick whichever of 20% or 30% leaves you above a 15% margin.

Then add a buffer for Offsite Ads

If any meaningful share of your orders come through Etsy's offsite advertising, that adds 15% of the whole order. On the $24.65 example, an Offsite Ads order drops profit from $9.86 to $6.16 — a 25% margin.

Two ways to handle it: price so that the Offsite Ads case is still acceptable, or opt out if you are under the $10,000 threshold. What you should not do is ignore it and discover it one sale at a time.

Free shipping or charged separately?

Etsy charges the transaction fee on shipping you charge, so the fee cost is identical either way. What changes is ranking and buyer behaviour: Etsy favours free shipping in search, and buyers convert better when shipping is included.

The honest version: fold shipping into the item price if you can absorb it, because you pay the same fees but get the search benefit. What you must not do is under-charge shipping — the shortfall comes straight out of profit, and you still pay the fee on what you did charge.

Quick answers

What margin should I aim for?

30–50% net after fees and materials for handmade and print-on-demand goods. Below 15% is fragile: one refund, one return, one Offsite Ads order and the sale is gone.

Should I price higher than competitors?

Only if you can say why in one sentence. Price is a signal, and on a marketplace full of similar photos it is often the only differentiator a buyer can read quickly.

What if I cannot hit a decent margin at a competitive price?

Then the product is the problem, not the price. Cheaper materials, a smaller format, or bundling to raise the average order value are the levers — lowering the price only shrinks the margin further.
